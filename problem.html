The problem

Ajo runs on trust. The record doesn’t.

Nothing is wrong with how ajo works. What breaks is the notebook, the group chat and the memory that everyone is asked to rely on.

Where it starts

The tools people are using today

Ajo — also called esusu or adashe — is a rotational savings system: a group contributes regularly and takes turns receiving the pooled amount. Most groups still track it like this.

  • A paper notebook kept by one coordinator
  • WhatsApp messages scrolled back through
  • A spreadsheet only one person can open
  • Bank-transfer screenshots
  • Calculations done by hand
  • Memory, when all of the above run out

What goes wrong

Six failures every coordinator recognises

Each one is small on its own. Together they cost the coordinator their time and cost the group its confidence.

01

Tracking who has paid

With ten members and a weekly contribution, that is forty entries a month to keep straight — by hand, in one book.

02

Forgotten deadlines

Nobody sets out to miss a contribution. They miss it because nothing reminded them before the day passed.

03

Miscalculated balances

One arithmetic slip carried forward for three cycles becomes an argument nobody can win from memory.

04

Lost physical records

A soaked, torn or misplaced notebook takes the group’s whole history with it. There is no second copy.

05

No transparency at scale

Five members can hold it in their heads. Past that, only the coordinator knows the real position — and everyone else has to take their word.

06

Disputes over payments

Two people remember the same Friday differently. Without a record, the disagreement is about character, not about money.

The real cost

Unclear records don’t just confuse. They erode trust.

Ajo runs on trust between people who often know each other well — family, neighbours, traders on the same street. When the record is unclear, suspicion fills the gap, and it attaches to people rather than to paperwork.

Members can’t confirm their own contribution history. The coordinator spends the cycle chasing payments and answering the same question instead of running the group. And a group that worked for years can fall apart over a disagreement that a single shared page would have settled in seconds.

What members lose

No way to prove what they paid, no view of the group’s position, and no idea what happens if they miss.

What coordinators lose

Hours every cycle spent reconciling, reminding and re-explaining — unpaid, and usually thankless.

What the group loses

The confidence to grow. Groups stay small because nobody can keep track past a handful of people.

Who this affects

Anyone saving together, anywhere

Market traders

Daily and weekly contributions, often in cash, with the highest volume of entries to track.

Artisans

Saving toward tools, materials or a workshop, with income that changes week to week.

Salary earners

Monthly contributions with colleagues, where a missed month is easy to overlook.

Family groups

Where a money dispute costs far more than the money, because the relationship comes first.

Our answer

Keep the ajo. Fix the record.

AjoFam doesn’t try to change how ajo works — the rotation, the trust and the rules stay exactly as your group set them. It replaces unreliable memory and scattered records with one transparent, shared page, so everyone can see the group’s status and whose turn it is at a glance.
